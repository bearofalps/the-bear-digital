Before that happened, I made a decision that appears irrational if the goal was simply to make the first laps easier.

I turned off the assistance.

Traction control off. Stability management off. Countersteer assistance off. Racing line off. Automatic transmission off. Manual from the start of the serious work. ABS stayed.

It was a decision about what I was there to rebuild.

If the game corrected the inputs I was trying to learn, I would receive a cleaner result and worse information. If traction control reduced the consequence of throttle, I would not know with enough resolution what my finger had asked from the rear tyres. If stability management rescued the rotation, I could finish more laps while remaining uncertain about which part of the lap belonged to me. If the transmission shifted itself, one entire timing channel would stay disconnected.

I did not need the game to demonstrate that the game could keep a car on the road. I needed it to tell me what my inputs produced.

ABS was different. In my working model, it did not replace the conversation I was trying to have with the chassis. It prevented one failure mode without editing out the driving style I was rebuilding. That was where I drew the line between support that preserved agency and intervention that quietly substituted for it.

This made the beginning harder. That was not an accidental side effect. It was also not punishment.

The fastest route to competence is not always the route with the least immediate resistance. Sometimes assistance lowers the visible error by hiding the channel you need to calibrate. In networking, a system can look stable because an upstream device is silently repairing what the downstream node keeps getting wrong. Remove the upstream correction and the fault suddenly appears. The fault was there before. Now it is available for diagnosis.

GT7 gave me the fault at full resolution.

Brake too late: miss the corner.

Turn while the weight is wrong: understeer.

Ask too much from the throttle: lose the rear.

Change gear at the wrong moment: interrupt the sequence you were trying to build.

No committee. No interpretation meeting. No performance review six months later. No social translation layer between action and consequence. The car either rotated or it did not.

For a few weeks, mostly it did not.

Then, increasingly, it did.

I did not call any of this recovery architecture at the time.

That language came later, after the documents, the comparisons, the long conversations and the habit of looking back at a sequence to find the mechanism inside it. In September, I was not performing a case study on myself. I was trying to drive.

This distinction is important because explanation has a bad habit of moving into an event and claiming it was there from the beginning. Once you can name a pattern, every earlier action starts looking like a deliberate expression of the mature theory. It was not. I had an intention—to plan the shutdown—and a working instinct about what kind of environment might hold me. I did not yet have the complete diagram.

The game supplied four things before I had proper names for them.

The first was low-friction access.

Controller. Screen. Drive.

A ten-minute session did not fail because it was only ten minutes. A two-hour session did not require scheduling, travel, another person’s availability or a public version of me. There was no setup debt large enough to make beginning irrational on a low-capacity evening. The same door worked at several energy levels.

This sounds small until beginning is the expensive part.

People who can reliably convert intention into action often treat friction as an inconvenience. When that conversion has become unstable, friction becomes architecture. Each required step is another place where the signal can die: find the equipment, prepare the equipment, leave the house, coordinate with someone, tolerate uncertainty, be observed beginning badly. An activity can be ideal in theory and still be unreachable because its entry path contains too many gates.

GT7 reduced the route to almost nothing. It did not remove the controller barrier. It placed the barrier close enough for repeated contact.

The second thing was structured freedom.

The track had walls, edges, braking zones, grip limits and a direction. The physics did not care about my biography. Inside those constraints sat a large field of choices: car, setup, line, gear, tyre, pace, risk, repetition. The rules were clear enough to orient me and the solution space was wide enough to remain alive.

That combination is rare. Too little structure and every possibility arrives as a demand to invent the task. Too much structure and the task becomes obedience. GT7 gave me a bounded system with enough depth to explore. The track told me where the problem was. It did not tell me how I had to become the driver capable of solving it.

The third thing was immediate causal feedback.

In ordinary life, especially in work built around systems and other people, the distance between an action and its result can become enormous. You can make the correct decision and watch the outcome be altered by timing, incentives, someone else’s state, an invisible dependency, or an assumption no one documented. You can make the wrong decision and receive no error until the season is live and the rain is horizontal. The feedback arrives late, mixed with other signals, and sometimes carrying politics.

On the track, the loop closed in milliseconds.

Input. Response.

Try again.

The fourth thing was density.

Driving was not one problem. It was perception, timing, planning, correction, risk, mechanical behaviour and adaptation running together. Later, the garage would add collection and comparison; tuning would add another complete diagnostic layer. At Level 1, there was already enough simultaneous information to occupy the part of my mind that becomes restless when a task is too thin.

Simple relaxation often does not relax me. A complex system that answers honestly can.

During the first planned descent, a racing simulation gave me clear boundaries, high informational density and fast causal feedback. I returned to it often enough for the interface to stop rejecting me. As that happened, a capacity I had considered inaccessible began to answer again.

That is observation.

The architecture is what I built from it later.

Once the controller clicked, progress stopped being a negotiation with the interface and became a conversation with the game.

Not an easy conversation. A legible one.

There is a difference. Easy gives you fewer demands. Legible lets you understand the demands you failed to meet. I did not need GT7 to lower the amount of reality in the loop. I needed reality to arrive in a form I could parse.

At first, every corner consumed the entire channel. Braking, turning, shifting and accelerating did not yet form one movement. They queued for attention. If one arrived late, the whole sequence broke. That kind of failure feels clumsy because it is clumsy, but clumsiness is not the same as incapacity. It is a routing problem under load.

Repeated laps began to change the routing.

The gear change moved out of the foreground. Steering correction stopped requiring a verbal instruction inside my head. I could enter a corner with enough unused bandwidth to notice what the car was doing between the obvious events. Instead of merely trying not to leave the track, I could feel the sequence: brake, release, load the front, ask for rotation, hold or change the request with throttle, let the car settle, use the exit.

The old knowledge had not transferred automatically, but neither had it disappeared. Once the new input layer became usable, the old model began finding routes through it.

That was the click.
