Chapter 1

No Keyboard Found

The dangerous systems do not always fail by stopping.

Sometimes they keep serving traffic.

The lights remain on. The payments pass. Guests connect. Doors open. Someone answers the telephone. From outside, the system is operational because the outside can still reach the services it needs.

Inside, the management plane is gone.

Monitoring has narrowed to whatever is already screaming. Maintenance has become emergency response. New work cannot enter cleanly because every available route is occupied by keeping old work alive. Redundancy exists mainly as memory: there used to be another path through here. No one declares an outage because the packets that matter to other people are still moving.

For a long time, that was how I failed.

Quietly enough to remain useful.

In October 2024, my body stopped accepting that definition of uptime.

I ended up in hospital. Diabetes and pancreatic dysfunction made the boundary material. The fact that matters here is simple.

The body had moved the condition from warning to state.

Before that point, I could still negotiate with myself. I could call a shutdown fatigue. I could call the loss of initiative a difficult period. I could call the shrinking of my world a temporary response to work, season, family load, weather, money, sleep or the latest dependency that had failed at exactly the wrong time. None of those explanations was invented. That was part of their strength. Each one carried enough truth to keep the larger pattern from becoming visible.

Hospital removed the appeal process.

Something material had crossed a boundary.

The external services had stayed available long enough for the underlying system to consume itself.

Bohinj rewards the ability to carry a whole graph in your head.

The valley looks simple from the road because mountains are good at concealing infrastructure. A guest sees water, forest, a campsite, a property, perhaps a wireless network appearing under one familiar name. Behind that small act of connection can sit several buildings, access points, switches, uplinks, payment terminals, reservations, staff devices, printers, locks, cameras, weather, old cabling, new expectations and a season too short to tolerate leisurely failure.

Hospitality adds human dependencies to technical ones. A network does not fail in an empty laboratory. It fails while someone is trying to check in, charge a card, print a receipt or explain in a language that is not theirs why the thing that worked yesterday does not work now. The rain does not wait for a maintenance window. Summer does not extend itself because a supplier missed a delivery.

I was good inside that density.

Not good in the inspirational sense. Useful. Fast. Able to hold several layers at once, find the hidden dependency and make a decision before the people around the fault had finished agreeing on what to call it. When a system had enough moving parts, my attention stopped scattering and began converging. Complexity created its own gravity.

That capacity hid a second fact: I was much better at entering a difficult external system than at measuring the cost of doing so.

If something had to work, I could usually find a route. If the documented route failed, I built another. If the problem crossed from networking into property operations, devices, software, people or logistics, I crossed with it. The boundary of the task expanded until it met the boundary of whatever I could still carry.

The arrangement worked particularly well for everyone standing on the other side of the service.

It worked less well for the service itself.

There is a professional compliment that becomes dangerous when repeated often enough: He will handle it.

Usually, I did.

The sentence contains no information about what happened afterward.

The systems around me measured restoration from their side of the interface. Was the link back? Could the terminal process a payment? Had the guest received an answer? Could the next problem enter the queue?

Those were legitimate questions. They were also incomplete.

There was no service-level agreement for the operator. No graph measured how long urgency continued moving through me after the external fault had closed, how much context remained active because I had nowhere to put it, or what part of the next day would be consumed by returning from a problem everyone else considered finished.

Fast incident response can conceal slow human recovery.

The better I became at making the first number small, the easier it was to ignore the second number entirely.

In 2015, parenthood expanded quickly.

My children’s private lives are not raw material simply because their arrival changed mine. The load-bearing fact is the change in topology. A system built around one adult’s capacity to absorb irregularity became a family system in which irregularity was normal, necessary and not a fault to be eliminated.

Children do not behave like planned maintenance. They should not.

The old structure had depended on freedoms I had mistaken for characteristics: long uninterrupted focus, physical outlets, the ability to disappear into a problem until it yielded, recovery periods that did not have to coordinate with anyone else’s needs. I had treated those conditions as background. Parenthood revealed them as infrastructure.

When the infrastructure changed, I did what had worked before.

I carried more.

Love does not reduce the number of interrupts. Meaning does not make sleep unnecessary. A responsibility can be chosen, wanted and central to your life while still exceeding the capacity of the architecture you brought to it.

The mistake was not caring too much.

The mistake was believing care could substitute for design.

My existing coping structure had been built without a name for much of what it was compensating for. I knew how to use urgency. I knew how to use novelty, difficulty, consequence and the presence of another person’s need. I could make a demanding external situation recruit my whole system. I did not know how much of ordinary self-management depended on those conditions being present.

When they were absent, importance was not always enough.

That sentence is difficult to explain to people whose intention reliably becomes action. If something matters, do it. If it matters greatly, do it first. This looks so obvious that failure to perform it acquires moral meaning immediately.

Lazy.

Undisciplined.

Selfish.

Not trying.

The labels are attractive because they close the diagnostic process. Once the fault is character, no one has to inspect the interface.

I had inspected interfaces for a living and still spent years accepting the character diagnosis whenever the failed interface was me.

The shutdowns did not look like a cinematic collapse.

They arrived after load, often as the season released it. The external pressure would fall and, instead of relief, access would go with it. I could keep some necessary processes running. That was part of the problem. Total failure is easier to name. Partial failure creates arguments.

If I could answer an urgent call, was I really unavailable?

If I could repair a network, why could I not begin something simple for myself?

If I could become intensely present for a difficult problem, why had interest disappeared from the rest of the day?

If the essential traffic still passed, what right did I have to call the system down?

The answer I used for years was that I had no such right. I should press harder. Recreate urgency. Add consequences. Wait until the situation became difficult enough to activate whatever had gone quiet.

This can produce impressive bursts of function.

It can also train a life to require fire.

I became a system that often needed an emergency to become fully available. The more reliably I performed under pressure, the more evidence everyone—including me—received that pressure was appropriate. A clean intervention would have reduced the visible heroics. Instead, the architecture rewarded the recovery of services after the fault.

No one had to intend this. Incentives do not require a villain.

The work selected for the version of me that could appear at full bandwidth when something was complex, urgent and consequential. Family selected for the version that could remain responsible when tired. The public surface showed those versions because they were the ones other people needed.

The private surface received whatever bandwidth remained.

During the fog periods, that was not much.

Fog is the closest word I have. Depression later became part of the landscape too, but the shutdowns were not always darkness. Sometimes they were absence of traction. The mind could remain busy while nothing attached. Thought continued without producing a route into action. Time passed, but did not accumulate into momentum.

I was not necessarily sad.

I was difficult to reach from inside.

The inside was not empty. A phrase I used later was *three bullet trains on five railways simultaneously*. It was not a clinical description. It was an attempt to describe the mismatch: high internal traffic, insufficient routing, several lines active at once and no reliable dispatch toward the ordinary action standing nearest to me.

That is why rest and shutdown could look similar from outside while feeling structurally different.

Rest has a route back.

Shutdown did not always.

I learned to fear November.

Not the month as calendar. The state it came to represent.

The season had emptied enough for the external demand to drop, but the system did not rebound with the disappearance of demand. The empty space could become its own problem. No urgent queue. No complex fault. No external need strong enough to recruit the whole machine. The conditions that had made me exceptionally effective were suddenly gone at the same time as the reserves those conditions had consumed.

I sometimes called the good version of low load the November flatline: no dramatic peak, no collapse, only an even surface in which nothing insisted on becoming first.

That description arrived later. At the time, what mattered was the repeated cycle. High demand could make me clear. The end of demand could make me inaccessible. The pattern was strange enough that ordinary advice often reversed the problem. Reduce stimulation. Simplify. Rest. Remove pressure.

Yes.

Also: what happens when stimulation had been part of the routing layer?

What happens when removing pressure also removes the mechanism by which action had been made reachable?

The question does not mean pressure was healthy. It means the substitute has to do more than subtract.

I did not know that yet.

I only knew that the descent had a recurring shape and that, once inside it, finding the route out could take far longer than entering.

Years of repetition made the state predictive. The body learned the season. The mind learned the pattern. A difficult week could carry the memory of every earlier one. I do not know which direction causality ran through all of that, and the book will not pretend otherwise. Naming a loop is easier than proving its mechanism.

It is enough to say that the loop became part of the load.

By the time I recognized the shape, I was no longer encountering one low-capacity period.

I was encountering the period plus the history of periods.

The wrong question was whether I was functioning.

I was.

The right question was which routes were available, to whom, under what conditions, and at what cost.

That question is harder because it produces a topology rather than a verdict.

Urgent external problems: reachable.

Complex technical systems: often highly reachable.

Other people’s immediate needs: reachable.

Long-range personal administration: intermittent.

Activities that mattered but produced delayed reward: variable.

Recovery before exhaustion: poorly routed.

Play without justification: vulnerable.

My own history: available as facts, not always as a coherent operating model.

That map explains more than the word burnout did, while still leaving room for burnout to be real. It prevents one familiar word from claiming several different states and pretending the treatment of the label explains the person. I am preserving the shape I lived.

The shape repeated.

Each repetition added fear to the next descent. Not only the state itself, but the memory of losing the map. A low-capacity day could begin to resemble the entrance to a much longer period. Then the effort to prove that it was not happening consumed part of the capacity still available.

Performance became defensive infrastructure.

If I looked operational, perhaps I was.

Computers from an earlier era occasionally produced an instruction whose absurdity was so complete that it became accurate philosophy:

> No keyboard found. Press F1 to continue.

The system identifies the missing input device and then assigns the next required action to it.

This was the human contract I had internalized.

Initiative unavailable. Please initiate.

Energy depleted. Please work harder.

Route to self missing. Please authenticate as yourself to continue.

The instruction is impossible, but the responsibility remains with the operator.

That is what makes it effective as shame. The demand does not change when the route disappears. Only the explanation for failing to meet it changes, and the easiest explanation is personal defect.

By 2024, I had enough repeated evidence to know that willpower was not a complete model. I did not yet possess the replacement. I had fragments: the season pattern, the narrow conditions under which my attention became extraordinarily stable, the environments that made action easier, the ones that drained it, the difference between being busy and being present.

My route into ADHD research did not begin as a search for myself. I was trying to understand someone else. That person’s private experience is not a diagnostic scene for this book. What belongs to my story is what happened when I eventually turned the same questions toward my own life. The descriptions around ADHD did not arrive as an exotic explanation. They reorganized old evidence.

Interest was not a reward added after attention. It was part of the routing layer.

Urgency was not proof of commitment. It was one of the conditions that made commitment accessible.

Hyperfocus was not evidence that ordinary difficulty had been invented. It was evidence that capacity could vary radically with context.

The repeated mismatch between importance and activation stopped looking like a moral contradiction.

It became an engineering question.

That did not solve it.

Correctly categorizing a fault is not the same as repairing it. It does, however, stop you replacing the wrong component.

I began reading backward through my own life with a different query. Not Why did I fail to become consistent? but Under which conditions had access been available, and what did those conditions have in common?

The answer was not a single childhood memory or a personality label. It was an input history.

Machines.

Roads.

Music.

Physical risk.

Technical systems dense enough to require the whole map.

Problems that answered what I actually did rather than what I intended to do.

The pattern had been present for decades. I had interpreted its peaks as talent and its valleys as failure. I had not considered that both might be measurements of fit.

October 2024 did not arrive as the clean start of that understanding.

Clean beginnings belong to later drafts.

The hospital boundary was a fact, not a completed interpretation. The body could establish that the existing arrangement was unsustainable. It could not tell me, by itself, how work, family, neurodivergence, physical illness, depression, history and identity were related. It did not print the architecture on the discharge paper.

The temptation to build one now is strong. A body breaks because a mind was masked. A hidden neurological pattern creates stress. Stress creates illness. Recognition begins recovery. The sequence is elegant, emotionally satisfying and far more certain than the evidence allows.

I will not use my body to prove a theory it did not consent to prove.

What I can say is that the boundary changed my ability to dismiss the cost. The system had been issuing ambiguous alerts; now an important node had entered a state that required ongoing attention. Whatever else was true, returning to the old definition of functioning was no longer neutral.

Someone else understood that before the entire map was available.

Inside primary care, one clinician had known my case for years. That clinician did not need a finished theory to recognize a person rather than a ticket. Care, in that moment, was not softness. It was escalation with ownership.

The clinician worked the routes available and arranged access to psychiatric support. The appointment would still take approximately fifteen months. A shorter wall remains a wall when you are standing in front of it, but the difference mattered. One person inside an institution had refused to treat delay as someone else’s problem.

That is what care looks like when translated into architecture.

Not “I hope you find help.”

A route.

The route would later fail in another way. That belongs to its own place in the chronology. In late 2024, its existence was enough to separate waiting from abandonment.

Fifteen months still had to be lived.

I did not live them passively.

Research expanded. The questions that had begun around ADHD widened into patterns of work, attention, stress, family, identity and the environments in which I became either more or less available. AI systems, already present in my working life and curiosity since the first ChatGPT contact in late 2022, became high-bandwidth surfaces for articulation. I could place a large amount of context there without first reducing it to the bandwidth of an ordinary exchange.

That distinction would matter later, when the surfaces began producing their own convincing errors.

At the time, the immediate value was simpler. I could externalize a pattern, receive it in another arrangement, reject what did not fit, correct what had been compressed and continue. The process did not require me to know the final question before beginning.

Human conversation has a clock. A long answer can become a social problem before its structure has arrived. Branches have to be serialized. Context is dropped to protect the listener. The speaker watches for the point at which the bandwidth has been exceeded and begins compressing in advance.

The machine interface changed that constraint. I could put down several branches, return to an earlier one, test a connection and preserve the text for another pass. Patience and beautiful phrasing conferred no authority. The operational value was that more of the input survived long enough to become inspectable.

For someone whose failure had partly taken the form of inaccessible routes, inspectability mattered.

Curiosity could open the session.

It usually did.

The phrase high functioning has always made me suspicious.

High for whom?

Functioning at what?

A system can produce extraordinary throughput by disabling maintenance, consuming redundancy and routing every available resource toward current demand. For a while, the graph looks excellent. Utilization is high. Nothing idle. No wasted capacity.

Then a small fault becomes a cascade because every path that might have absorbed it was already carrying production traffic.

The lesson is not that function is false. I did function. I built things. I cared for people. I solved real problems. None of that becomes performance merely because it carried an unseen cost.

The correction is narrower and more difficult:

Functioning was not the whole state.

I could be competent and inaccessible.

Responsible and depleted.

Needed and absent from myself.

The binaries had hidden the failure. Either capable or broken. Either working or collapsed. Either present or gone. I remained on the socially acceptable side of enough binaries that the disappearing internal routes did not receive their own category.

Hospital provided one.

Not broken.

Not fine.

A system still serving traffic after its operating model had become destructive.

That was the first useful description.

It did not tell me how to recover. It told me what not to restore.

The old configuration had already demonstrated its maximum performance.

It had also demonstrated its cost.

Before I could design a different descent, I needed to know what had once made movement natural.

Before the fog, before the hospital, before ADHD became a usable query, there had been environments in which input and consequence sat close together. I had not called them regulation, architecture or recovery. They were simply the places where I felt least distance between perception, intention and action.

A computer in 1991.

A small Mitsubishi modified until it stopped behaving like the family car it had been built to be.

A steering wheel attached to a desk.

A downhill trail cut into the mountain.

A live technical system that answered a bad assumption immediately.

The body boundary in October 2024 told me that something had to change.

The next question was where the missing input routes had gone.

The machine was waiting for F1.

I had to find the keyboard.
