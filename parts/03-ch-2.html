Chapter 2

The Inputs

The first keyboard arrived in 1991.

So did the first PC.

The early software inventory was small enough to remember by name: Prince of Persia, Tetris, Test Drive. Three different machines hidden inside one beige machine. Timing and movement. Pattern and consequence. A road represented by pixels and accepted as a road because the input changed what happened next.

I was thirteen when 1991 began and fourteen before it ended. The computer did not explain me to myself. It did something more useful.

It answered.

Press a key. Receive a state change.

Fail. Restart.

Try another sequence.

The rules did not become simple merely because they were explicit. Tetris has almost nothing to explain and no end-state in which the system congratulates you for understanding it. The space becomes harder to manage because of the history of your own decisions. Prince of Persia turns timing into a physical language while the body remains in a chair. Test Drive offers the impossible compression of a car into a keyboard and still preserves enough relationship between command and consequence to create attention.

The keyboard was not realistic steering.

It was available.

That trade would repeat through my life long before I learned to describe it.

An interface did not have to reproduce reality perfectly. It had to preserve the part of the relationship that mattered: I do this; the system does that; now I know something I did not know before.

The shorter that distance became, the more present I became inside it.

My first car had a name.

Mici.

It was a Mitsubishi Colt Lancer 1.5, not the silver rally weapon that would later dominate my imagination. It left the factory as a small family car with approximately eighty-seven horsepower and no known ambition to become a physics laboratory for a teenager in Bohinj.

I supplied the ambition.

The later record lists the changes with the precision of a parts invoice remembered by someone for whom parts were never only parts. The original tall 13-inch tyres gave way to 195/50 R15 Dunlop SP Sport 3000s. JAMEX springs lowered the car by three centimetres. The dampers were made harder. The car became lower, wider at the contact patch, faster to answer and less willing to protect the driver from the surface beneath it.

This was not a large-power build. I could not solve the problem by purchasing a different engine or a different car. The available variables were grip, weight transfer, response and commitment.

So I changed those.

The philosophy was simple and, as a public-road safety principle, indefensible:

> Don’t brake. The brakes can’t handle the speed. The springs, shocks and tyres do.

That sentence contains youthful madness and a real mechanical model. It should not be cleaned into one or dismissed as the other.

The madness was obvious. I was asking a modest car on public roads to carry inputs beyond the use its manufacturer had designed around. Better tyres do not repeal consequences. Harder suspension does not turn a road into a closed circuit. Commitment is not a moral virtue when the cost can arrive through another person.

The model underneath it was that braking was not the only way to manage speed and direction. Load the front. Use the contact patch. Let weight transfer help the car rotate. Enter with enough commitment that the chassis remains part of the decision instead of becoming an object dragged behind fear.

The car answered honestly enough to charge for every lesson.

Front control arms became consumables. I kept spares available because the operating method had a known hardware cost. Tyres disappeared quickly. The modification did not create free performance; it moved the invoice.

That is one of the earliest patterns I can see clearly in retrospect. I was not satisfied by the manufacturer’s intended interface between driver and machine. I changed the interface until the result carried more information. Then I accepted maintenance as part of the architecture.

Accepted may be too mature a word for the teenager involved.

I paid it, anyway.

The car also carried a Nakamichi head unit whose relationship with imperfect compact discs was close to religious exclusion. Original disc or nothing. Beautiful sound, high standards, magnificent stubbornness. The device behaved as though error correction was a form of moral compromise.

I understood it immediately.

We were young. Cars had become fast enough to exceed the judgment available to the people buying them. Rural life made driving less optional than an urban account often understands. Public transport did not form a usable night-time system. Social life, alcohol, distance and machinery occupied the same graph.

The graph was dangerous.

I drove other cars from that period. A Clio Williams could make speed feel like something the chassis had decided to do with or without you. A Golf VR6 carried its power with more composure. A Peugeot 405 Mi16 belonged to another variation of the same European experiment: put serious mechanical capability inside a shape that could still pass through ordinary life as a car.

The differences mattered. Horsepower alone told almost nothing about the relationship. Wheelbase, weight, engine delivery, driven wheels, suspension and the willingness of the chassis to announce its movement changed who was actually in charge.

Some cars let the driver build a decision.

Some presented a decision already moving too quickly to revise.

I did not have those sentences then. I had hands on a steering wheel and a body learning which signals arrived before the obvious one.

This is how much technical knowledge enters: not as theory followed by application, but as consequence followed by attention. The rear becomes light. The steering changes weight. The road surface alters the available answer. The driver survives the mistake, asks why, and carries the result into the next corner.

Consequence was close.

The car returned the input through the steering, the seat, the tyres, the noise, the maintenance bill and sometimes fear. There was little room to confuse intention with result.

I valued that before I knew I did.

Running beneath the road years was another reference system.

World Rally Championship footage arrived with cars that seemed to solve the same problem at a level approaching art. Tommi Mäkinen won four consecutive drivers’ titles with Mitsubishi from 1996 through 1999. The silver Lancer Evolution became more than a desirable car. It was a demonstration that speed did not have to mean noise without intelligence.

Commitment could be exact.

A rally car enters conditions designed to invalidate certainty. Gravel changes. Grip moves. The road is only partly known and never fully stable. The driver and co-driver do not eliminate uncertainty. They build a protocol capable of moving through it at speed.

That was the attraction.

Not domination of the road. Coordination with changing information.

The Mäkinen-era Evo sat at the point where an ordinary road-going object still carried visible ancestry from the competition machine. It suggested that the category boundary could be crossed: family-shaped metal, disciplined into another state through engineering.

Mici became the first sketch of the Evo in my head. Mechanically absurd. Aspirationally exact. The present object pointed toward a possible one.

The later Library documents pulled my first PC, my gaming years and the Logitech MOMO Force wheel into one clean fourteen-year line beginning in 1991.

It is a good example of how machines manufacture coherence from related facts.

There was a PC in 1991.

There were games from then onward.

There was serious sim racing later.

There was a Logitech wheel, pedals and a gearshift.

These facts belong to one lineage. They do not belong to one date.

The cable between them must remain visible.

By the later sim-racing period, the input surface had changed completely. A keyboard tells the car left or right. A wheel allows angle. Pedals allow pressure and travel. A separate shift input lets the body distribute tasks across limbs instead of asking a few fingers to multiplex the whole machine.

The software became less forgiving too. F1 99–02 and its later mods demanded precision through long sequences. A mistake could begin several corners before the place it became visible. Richard Burns Rally made weight transfer unavoidable. On a loose surface, hesitation does not preserve safety in the simple way an outsider expects. A half-committed input can leave the car without the rotation or stability the chosen line requires.

Again: this was simulation. I will not ask a consumer physics model to certify my understanding of a real car. What the simulator could do was return internally consistent consequences quickly enough for patterns to accumulate.

Brake here.

Release now.

Move the weight.

Commit.

The wheel made those repetitions legible through a grammar the body already knew from the road. The road supplied consequence without repeatability. The simulation supplied repeatability without the same consequence. Together, they trained attention toward the interval before the obvious event.

Different games isolated different parts of the loop. The Formula racing years stretched precision across long, fast sequences. Rally simulations punished the idea that one stable technique could survive every surface. Both demanded inputs that were not only correct in magnitude but correct in timing.

Timing became another form of truth.

A late correct input is still late.

The downhill bike removed another layer of abstraction.

A bicycle is simple enough to carry and complex enough to disappear beneath attention. Two wheels, brakes, suspension, tyres, geometry, a trail and gravity. The input was no longer hands and feet operating a representation while the body remained safe in a chair. The body joined the packet.

I rode trails and helped build them in Bohinj. Building changes the relationship with terrain. A rider receives the line as a problem. A builder has already participated in the question: where speed will accumulate, where water will move, which root becomes structure and which becomes hazard, what a rider will see before committing to what is hidden.

The trail is an interface made from land.

It can communicate honestly or badly. A good line gives enough information for commitment without removing consequence. A bad one can surprise the rider for reasons that have nothing to do with skill. The design appears in the body of someone moving through it.

That is architecture at its least rhetorical.

If the drainage is wrong, rain edits the trail.

If the geometry is wrong, the wheel edits the rider.

No mission statement is consulted.

The whole body made downhill valuable. Attention could not retreat into commentary while the terrain continued. Perception, decision and action had to occupy almost the same moment. Presence was not calm. It was low distance between signal and response.

That definition would return much later through music, driving, relationships and institutions. At the time, it was a line through trees.

The other major input surface looked nothing like a trail.

I managed technical systems in a casino.

A casino is a dense argument about trust conducted through machines. Money, identity, access, surveillance, accounting, availability and regulation occupy the same environment. A device can be technically alive and operationally useless. A network can pass traffic while the application depending on it fails. A small timing or configuration problem can become a human problem immediately because the system is not moving abstract data. It is moving claims about value.

The work suited me for reasons I did not yet organize into a theory.

It was complex.

The consequence was real.

The system produced telemetry.

Several technical layers had to be held at once, and no single vendor’s explanation could be trusted to contain the whole fault. The job was not to memorize the official boundary of each component. It was to find the actual boundary across which the failure travelled.

That is still my native unit of thought: not the object, but the relationship.

A switch matters because of what connects through it. A firewall rule matters because of the traffic it permits, denies or misclassifies. A database matters because of which state another system expects it to preserve. A person in an organization matters not as a replaceable node but as a carrier of context, judgment and trust that may exist nowhere in the formal diagram.

The diagram is useful.

The undocumented routes usually decide whether the system survives contact with reality.

Casino work also made one difference between machines and institutions impossible to ignore. A technical system can be difficult without pretending that it is simple. Human systems often conceal their complexity behind titles and procedures. The official path says one thing; the functional path travels through the person who knows whom to call, which dependency is historically strange, where the documentation stopped matching the installation, and which alarm can wait five minutes without becoming ten hours of recovery.

I became very good at carrying those invisible paths.

Useful again.

Expensive again.

The work rewarded the ability to absorb context without always rewarding the work of externalizing it. If the system could be restored because I remembered the missing connector, the immediate problem was solved. The future continuity problem remained.

That would become another repeated theme: a functioning node can hide a weak architecture by being unusually capable.

The node receives praise.

The system retains the dependency.

Later, hospitality IT across Bohinj widened the graph.

The technical categories became less important than the service path. Network, point of sale, property device, access, software, guest expectation, staff workflow. The fault followed no respect for professional scope, so neither could the diagnosis.

This was systems work in the literal sense. Not because I used the word systems, but because the desired outcome crossed components owned by different people and understood at different resolutions.

The guest does not care which layer failed.

The packet does not care which supplier has the support contract.

Reality is rude about organizational boundaries.

I liked that rudeness.

It removed performance from the final test. A beautiful explanation could not route traffic. A confident vendor could not negotiate with link state. A values statement could not make an abandoned dependency cared for.

The infrastructure either carried the relationship or it did not.

This is where care became technical for me long before I called it an architectural property. A well-built system does not merely perform under ideal load. It protects the people depending on it from faults they did not create and cannot diagnose. It preserves a recovery path. It refuses some dangerous states. It exposes enough truth for another operator to continue.

Care is not kindness applied after the engineering.

It is visible in what the engineering allows to happen under pressure.

I understood that clearly in networks.

I was slower to apply it to myself.

Looking backward, the inputs form a suspiciously coherent story.

Computer.

Car.

Simulation.

Downhill.

Casino.

Networks.

Each environment is dense. Each returns consequence. Each rewards context. Each reduces the distance between what is claimed and what actually happens.

It would be easy to call this a fixed native signature present from childhood and preserved without change.

That would be another clean story.

The truth is less obedient. I changed. Skills accumulated unevenly. Some environments selected for strengths while allowing weaknesses to remain invisible. Some preferences were built by repetition rather than revealed as essence. Dangerous feedback can be honest and still be dangerous. High-stakes work can produce presence while quietly making lower-stakes life inaccessible.

The pattern is real.

Its meaning was not settled in advance.

What I can say is that I repeatedly moved toward systems where the interface mattered and the answer could not be negotiated after the fact. I trusted a machine more when it exposed my error than when it manufactured a cleaner result. I trusted people more when stated value survived contact with cost. I became restless inside tasks too thin to recruit the whole map and exhausted inside complexity whose rules kept changing invisibly.

The best environments were not easy.

They were legible.

The worst were not always difficult.

They were incoherent.

That distinction explains more of my history than any claim that I simply liked challenge.

Challenge without signal is noise.

Signal without enough complexity becomes repetition.

The operating range sat where density, feedback, consequence and curiosity met.

That does not make every high-consequence environment good for me. A public road can provide immediate feedback and still be the wrong place to seek it. Emergency work can recruit my whole attention and still train the surrounding system to manufacture emergencies. Physical risk can produce presence while charging a price no useful architecture should require.

The pattern is not an instruction to maximize danger.

It is an instruction to preserve signal.

The safer interfaces mattered precisely because they could keep the relationship while changing the cost. Simulation retained input, adaptation and consequence without asking a road user to participate in my learning. A trail made design and terrain physical, but could be revisited and repaired. A technical test environment could expose a dependency before a guest encountered it. The best replacement was not the one that reproduced the highest stakes. It was the one that preserved enough truth at lower stakes for attention to remain present.

For years, life accidentally supplied enough of that range to keep me moving. Cars supplied it. Games supplied it. Trails supplied it. Work supplied it in concentrated bursts. When one channel ended, another often appeared.

Then several narrowed together.

The sim rig went into storage around 2005 or 2006. Downhill later disappeared from ordinary life. Family and work consumed the remaining irregular spaces. Technical density remained available, but increasingly as responsibility rather than exploration. The input system became unbalanced: plenty of external demand, very few interfaces that returned me to myself.

The old knowledge did not announce whether it had survived.

There was nowhere obvious for it to answer.

By the time I began thinking seriously about Gran Turismo 7, I had decades of input history and no usable route into the device the game placed in my hands.

The first keyboard had taught me that an imperfect interface could still open a world.

The first controller appeared determined to prove the opposite.
