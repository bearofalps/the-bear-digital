The photographs were published in 2013. They cannot be used as a time machine for every surface, light or decoration I knew in 1995. What they preserve is the spatial intention: levels looking into levels, balconies and stairs, sculptural bodies, reflective ceilings, colour refusing to stay on one plane.

The blocks in the empty-room photographs look almost like furniture until a person supplies scale. They were kocke: enormous concrete subwoofer enclosures, with the drivers on one face and four sides in concrete. Four of them marked the corners of the dancefloor, itself effectively in the centre of the room. Several smaller ones were distributed through the rest of the venue.

The floor was almost absurdly simple. It was not a stage or an intricate platform, only a field of different tiles inside an industrial hall. Four concrete masses defined its corners. Then light, sound and bodies converted it into somewhere else.

Simple did not mean accidental. The tile boundary, the placement of the kocke, the sightlines, mezzanine, lights, decoration, entry ritual and progression of the night belonged to one composition. No piece created PLUR. Together, they made the warehouse more capable of carrying the state we brought into it.

The sound system was not equipment placed into the architecture. Its acoustic mass was architecture, arranged around bodies rather than projected at them from one distant edge.

Even empty, the room does not recede into background.

It performs back.

PLUR means Peace, Love, Unity, Respect.

Four words can become a slogan in less time than it takes to print them on a shirt.

That is not what I mean.

I use PLUR for a repeated social state I experienced directly: people arriving with ordinary guards, identities and divisions, then becoming more available to one another inside a shared environment of music, architecture, expectation, chemistry, movement and peer behaviour.

The acronym did not cause the state.

It named what the state felt organized around.

Peace: threat reduced enough for another person to stop being an obstacle.

Love: not romance, not indiscriminate intimacy, but warmth allowed into the public space.

Unity: difference remaining while the room acquires a shared direction.

Respect: the boundary that keeps unity from becoming consumption.

Those definitions are mine now. On the floor, the system did not arrive as a lecture.

It arrived as behaviour.

I cannot honestly compress those years into one representative night. What survives is recurring-pattern memory: not a transcript of one Friday, but the weekly oscillator my body still knows.

The event did not begin at the door.

Monday was for sleeping and licking the wounds of the weekend before. By Tuesday, the next one had already started. Flyers moved through cities and across road junctions in the hands of girls wearing the scene before most of Slovenia had language for it: bright hair, enormous platforms, very little clothing. The flyers were information, art, collectible proof of belonging and part of the admission system. No flyer, no pass.

By Friday, anticipation had been accumulating for days. I locked the small shop shortly after seven and Bohinj lifted off toward Izola.

The drive south was part of the event. Music inside the car carried us for roughly two hours. Then, parked beneath the bright red Droga sign, we turned it off.

We wanted to hear the building.

Across the parking lot came a pulse that was not yet music. I remember it below ordinary listening, a low-frequency pressure from the system warming inside the warehouse. As we moved closer, the bass became more physical while the track itself remained trapped behind the walls.

Above it, the aluminium roof rattled its own vibe.

That was how we knew it was coming.

The parking lot filled while more cars kept arriving from Slovenia, Croatia and Italy. Faces reappeared. I knew many of them only from Gavioli, yet recognition required almost no biography. Drinks passed. Smiles and nods worked as routing protocols. The logos circulating that weekend—Mitsubishis, blue dolphins, stars and others—were read with the same minimum words and maximum shared context.

At eleven-something the crowd began moving toward the entrance.

The line bent through metal rails beneath the mezzanine windows. At its edges, a few people were still hunting for the object that made entry possible.

Then the routine panic: Fuck. Flyers.

Someone always had one.

As I remember the system, the flyer went in and a card marked with dots came out. Cash did not circulate inside. Consumption accumulated on the card and was settled at the exit. Lose it and the price became 20,000 Slovenian tolars. No money and no card meant cleaning the place.

That short transaction was the final ordinary checkpoint.

Then the floor opened.

The warm-up DJ played until midnight. Nobody particularly cared who he was. At twelve, the guest took over and the party lifted, but not all the way. Two hours later came the resident heavy artillery: Uroš Umek, Tine—Valentino Kanzyani—or both together. The main floor remained techno. The guests could be famous. We had travelled for the closing earthquake.

At around two, the room changed operating state.

The floor packed body against body. The system went to full pressure. Umek dropped the first hanging bass and bodies rearranged their organs, inside and outside.

Why was the light show suddenly so wild?

Substances belong inside that sentence. So do the smiles, nods, movement, music, room, expectation and thousands of small decisions not to turn another person into a threat. Remove the chemistry and the account lies. Reduce the whole state to chemistry and it lies again.

“PLUR was in the air” is too clean for the memory.

PLUR was in the nose, mixed with the vapour of sweat. You did not smell it. You felt it.

In the room’s arithmetic, two thousand became one and one became no one. That is not a verified head count or a universal report from everyone present. It is the surviving scale of my subjective experience: the ordinary boundary around me became temporarily less important than the moving human field.

Then sunlight came through those same mezzanine windows.

We had just arrived.

It was eight in the morning.

This sequence is not one reconstructed night. It is the repeated architecture through which those years survive in me: recovery, anticipation, flyers, departure, parking, distant pressure, roof, queue, card, corridor, floor, midnight, two, impact, sunlight, repeat. I will not assign every remembered object to one date or pretend every Friday and Saturday followed an identical schedule. The pattern is more honest than a cinematic composite and more alive than a chronology stripped of the body.

I saw it repeatedly.

I lived inside it.

Peers recognized it then and continued arguing about it later.

It remained distinct enough that, years afterward, I could detect weaker versions without needing the original chemistry.

That is lived evidence, not a randomized trial.

That is the lived claim. The scientific one must stay narrower.

MDMA and other substances could reduce fear, soften defensive control, increase subjective closeness and make social contact easier.

Science supports parts of that account at a general level. Controlled studies and reviews report changes in sociability, closeness and some measures of emotional empathy. They do not produce one simple result across trust, cooperation, empathy and durable prosocial behaviour. Illicit pills and powders also carried risks, uncertain contents and consequences that the language of love did not cancel.

Those findings make chemistry part of the environment, not its full architecture.
