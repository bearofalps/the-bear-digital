The cable length matters.

There was early gaming. There was later serious sim racing. Then there was a hard stop. Downhill mountain biking and technical work carried other parts of the same appetite for years: speed, feedback, risk, changing conditions, decisions whose quality became visible. Then those routes narrowed too. By the time GT7 entered the recovery plan, I was not resuming a hobby paused last winter. I was asking a channel closed for roughly two decades to carry live traffic again through different hardware.

No wonder the first packets dropped.

What surprised me was not that the old system could still recognize a corner. What surprised me was the patience required to let it be bad at transmission.

Patience has never been the glamorous part of commitment. We like commitment when it looks like force: brake late, turn in, hold the line. But staying with an interface that makes you look less capable than you are demands another kind. There is no adrenaline in mapping a gear change to a finger until the finger stops requiring supervision. There is only repetition and the refusal to treat temporary incompetence as a verdict.

The controller taught me that before it taught me speed.

Or, more precisely, the controller exposed the requirement. It did not care whether I learned it.

I had always preferred input systems with physical honesty. A keyboard gives a discrete event. A wheel gives angle. A pedal gives travel and resistance. A good network gives link state, counters, logs, paths. The controller initially felt like a compression algorithm throwing away the information I considered essential.

Then my use of it changed.

The tiny range did not grow. My resolution inside that range did. The trigger did not become a pedal. My finger learned to treat it as more than a switch. The device remained physically inferior to the full wheel-and-pedal grammar in obvious ways, but it stopped being illegible. I could pick it up and be driving before a larger setup would have finished making its case for why I should start tomorrow.

That trade was central to the shutdown architecture. Fidelity is useless when its activation cost makes it unavailable.

A full rig might have been the more accurate interface and the worse recovery system. It would require space, setup, posture, commitment before the first lap. The controller asked me to sacrifice one kind of precision in exchange for access. Then it made me earn new precision inside the compromise.

This was not settling.

It was context-appropriate engineering.

By December, the cleanest evidence was ordinary.

I kept coming back.

Not every session was significant. Most should not be made significant after the fact. There were races, cars, licences, credits, mistakes, retries. There was the pleasure of a machine beginning to answer with enough detail that I could distinguish its character from my error. There was curiosity about the next car and the same car again. There were evenings when the loop held and no philosophy was required.

That ordinariness is part of why it worked.

An activity cannot carry recovery if it must become a ceremony each time it is used. It cannot remain low friction if every session has to prove that the architecture is valid. The game was allowed to be a game. I was allowed to enjoy it without converting enjoyment into evidence of productivity.

The analysis came because analysis is what I do when a pattern repeats loudly enough. It did not have to supervise every lap.

Looking back, the movement from Level 1 to Level 43 gave me a measurable line through months that might otherwise have blurred. It did not tell the whole truth about those months. No metric does. But it contradicted one feared outcome: I had not gone fully offline.

I was operating at lower capacity, sometimes quiet, sometimes slow, not transformed into a new person and not returned to some untouched earlier version. Yet I was learning. The distinction is structural. A system that can learn is not merely preserving output. It is modifying itself in contact with feedback.

The earlier driver had not been deleted during the years away. That is the romantic way to say it, and I think it is partly right. The less romantic version is that some skills and preferences remained while access to their old interfaces disappeared. GT7 did not excavate a complete younger Marko from storage. It gave the present one a place to test what still answered.

Plenty did.

Commitment answered.

Pattern recognition answered.

The need for a system dense enough to become quiet answered.

The refusal to accept a managed result when an honest error was available answered.

Curiosity answered first and kept answering.

This is the part I trust most. Not because curiosity is always wise. It is perfectly capable of opening useless doors and following a signal into a wall. Its value is more basic. Curiosity keeps the channel bidirectional. It sends something into the environment and remains available for what comes back. It is difficult to be entirely gone while genuinely asking, What happens if I try this?

That was the September protocol, stripped of every later framework:

Try this.

Again.

Change the input.

Again.

Notice what came back.

Stay for one lap.

Then another.

The game had been available before September. The console had been available. The controller had been available. Even my knowledge had been available in the limited sense that it still existed somewhere in the system.

The difference was not possession.

It was sustained contact.

Sustained does not mean continuous. That distinction would become important far beyond the game. A working system is not one that never pauses, drops a packet or loses rhythm. It is one with a return path that remains cheaper than abandonment. September did not remove interruption. It made interruption less final. Each new session was not evidence that I had maintained an unbroken state. It was evidence that the route could be used again.

“The rest is history” is what we say when the later sequence becomes too large to carry in one sentence.

In this case the later sequence includes levels, cars, tracks, setup sheets, fuel maps, long stints, pauses that did not become disappearances, and a yellow machine that refused to behave until I found the syntax error in its differential. It includes the return of older techniques through new hardware and the discovery that the cars themselves did not all answer me in the same language.

Some were fast and left me outside the process.

Some made every command visible.

Some corrected me so smoothly that I could win without knowing which part of the win was mine.

Some punished ambiguity and became trustworthy because of it.

That difference would become one of the strongest signatures in the whole project: cars that listen and cars that manage.

But that comes next.

At Level 1, before I could judge the machine, I had to become audible to it.

The game did not open because I finally wanted it enough. It did not open because the correct console arrived. It opened slowly, after failed contacts, when a planned low-capacity season gave me enough repeated access to stay with the wrong interface until it became usable.

Weeks of struggle. Then the click.

A route.

The first lap completed.

Then the next honest input.
