Chapter 3

Level 1

The truthful version does not begin at Level 1.

It begins at lap zero.

For years I had been thinking about Gran Turismo 7. Thinking about it is cheap. Thinking preserves every possibility because it does not require the first corner. Eventually I loaded the game on a PlayStation 4. Later I loaded it on a PlayStation 5. There were a few attempts. I did not complete the first lap.

The game was not the problem. The controller was.

I knew what a car was doing before it finished doing it. I knew weight transfer, throttle, braking, rotation, the moment a front end begins to wash out and the narrower moment before the rear decides it has received a different instruction from the driver. Those inputs had accumulated across real cars, rally obsession, years of PC driving, a Logitech MOMO Force wheel, pedals, a gearshift, F1 99–02 and its later mods, Richard Burns Rally. The vocabulary was already in my body.

None of it came out through my thumbs.

A steering wheel gives you an arc. Your hands travel through space. A pedal pushes back along a line the leg understands. Even when the simulation is imperfect, the interface uses the grammar of the machine it represents. The controller compressed steering, throttle, braking, gear selection and correction into a few millimetres of stick travel and the movement of fingers that had never been assigned those jobs. I could read what needed to happen on the screen and still fail to send the command.

The software had loaded. I had not.

Attempt. Abort. Leave it.

Later, another attempt. Same problem. Before lap one was finished, the controller had already won the argument.

This is less elegant than the version that later appeared in some of the documents: September 2025, first console, first session, Level 1, heroic restart. That version has a clean boot sequence. It also did not happen. PS4 came before PS5. The game had been sitting at the edge of my attention for years. The first contacts failed. By the time I began seriously, I already had evidence that wanting the game and having the game were not enough.

That correction matters. Recovery stories are greedy for clean beginnings. They reach backward from whatever eventually worked and edit hesitation into preparation. They turn a series of aborted connections into destiny waiting for the correct date. The result is narratively efficient and mechanically false.

In my case, lap zero is the mechanism. The useful environment already existed. The stored knowledge already existed. The desire existed. What did not exist was a functioning route between them.

I would spend September building that route.

The summer season in Bohinj does not end because a calendar says autumn has begun. It releases pressure in stages.

Hospitality systems compress work into a narrow operating window. Properties fill. Networks, payment systems, access, devices, people and weather form temporary dependencies that behave as though they have always existed and will remain that way forever. Then the traffic changes. Guests thin out. Seasonal urgency begins to loosen. The external system steps down from peak load.

My internal system had a less graceful shutdown procedure.

For years the pattern had been simple in retrospect and almost impossible to see while inside it. I would carry the season. Then, at some point after carrying it, access would disappear. Not necessarily the capacity to keep a few essential processes alive. That was part of the camouflage. I could still be present enough to look operational from outside while losing the route to initiative, interest and the earlier versions of myself that had once moved without negotiation.

The metaphor I found later was an old boot error:

> No keyboard found. Press F1 to continue.

The absurdity is exact. The system asks for the input device it has just declared unavailable. You remain responsible for continuing, but the route by which you could continue is the missing component.

These periods were not planned rest. Rest has a relationship to return. This was closer to fog: time passing without producing reliable traction. I did not enter it by choice, and I could not schedule the point at which the controls would come back online. Each cycle made the next one easier to fear. Once a pattern has repeated enough times, the anticipation becomes part of the load.

By late summer 2025, I did not have a cure, a diagnosis capable of organizing the whole life, or a finished theory. I had a better map than before. I had spent enough time following ADHD research, then turning the same questions toward myself, to stop interpreting every loss of capacity as a moral failure. I could see that the seasonal descent was real whether I approved of it or not.

So I made one change.

I stopped treating the descent as an ambush.

When the season slowed in September, I began what I understood as my first planned recovery shutdown. The language sounds more technical than the act felt. I was not executing a perfect protocol. I was trying to prevent the usual sequence from completing itself. The aim was not to stay at summer capacity. That would have been another refusal to read the telemetry. The aim was to descend without falling through every layer of myself on the way down.

I needed an activity floor: something available when work capacity was low, demanding enough to hold attention, light enough to begin without mobilizing an expedition, and honest enough to answer an input immediately. No waiting for another person. No travel. No equipment ritual. No obligation to be impressive or useful. Ten minutes had to count. Two hours had to be possible. Stopping had to be allowed.

GT7 was already there.

So was the controller problem.

The choice in September was not to discover the game. It was to return to the failed interface with enough intent to stay past lap zero.

I was forty-seven. I would turn forty-eight on 23 November. This is not old, unless you are fourteen. It is old enough to possess several complete generations of muscle memory and young enough to be annoyed when none of them impress a gamepad.

I started again.

The first weeks were a negotiation between knowledge and transmission.

On the old wheel, steering was a movement of the hands around a physical centre. The pedals separated throttle and brake into different legs and different pressure gradients. A gearshift occupied its own space. I did not have to remember that these were separate channels. The hardware enforced the separation.

The controller offered no such courtesy. Steering lived under one thumb. Throttle and brake had moved to fingers. Gear changes occupied buttons close enough to other functions that knowledge alone could not prevent the wrong movement arriving at the wrong time. The car demanded coordinated inputs at speed from a device my body still treated as a small plastic argument.

I was not learning what a racing line was. I was learning how to tell the game that I knew.

That distinction kept the struggle interesting and made it infuriating. A complete novice can accept novice results as part of the contract. I had the opposite problem: internal expectation built on an interface that no longer existed. The eyes recognized the event. The mind issued a command in the old language. The thumbs returned a mistranslation.

I do not have one clean first spin preserved as the symbol for all the others. The evidence is blunter: a few early attempts did not survive the first lap, and when the serious start came, the controller remained a major problem for weeks.

Weeks matter.

“Then it clicked” is the kind of phrase that makes a process sound instantaneous. The click was real, but it was not magic and it was not one illuminated lap.
